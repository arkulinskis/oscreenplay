Screenplay format in ten minutes

A screenplay is a blueprint, not a novel: it describes only what the audience will see and hear, in a strict layout that lets anyone estimate the running time from the page count. Six elements do almost everything.

Scene heading

Also called a slugline. INT. or EXT., the place, then the time. A new heading every time the place or time changes.

INT. HARBOUR OFFICE - NIGHT

Action

What we see and hear, in the present tense. Short paragraphs, three or four lines at most. Introduce a character in CAPS with their age, once.

NORA KESSEL (29) stands at the window, coat still on. She is counting boats.

Character

The speaker's name, in caps, above their line. Extensions in brackets: (V.O.) voice-over, (O.S.) off-screen, (CONT'D) continuing.

NORA (V.O.)

Parenthetical

A short note on how a line is said, or a tiny action. Use rarely: trust the actor.

GUS
(not looking up)
Sit.

Dialogue

What they say. Under the name, in a narrow column, so a page of talk reads fast.

ELI
Is that a good point or a bad point?

Transition

How we leave a scene: CUT TO:, SMASH CUT TO:, DISSOLVE TO:. Modern scripts use very few; a new scene heading already means a cut.

SMASH CUT TO:

A page, put together

EXT. KESSEL DOCKS - WEDDING RECEPTION - DAY
A brass band. A hundred fishermen in borrowed suits. MARTA KESSEL (34), radiant and already a little drunk, dances with everyone except her new husband.
MARTA
(close, a whisper)
Look who came home. Don't sit in it. It's not yours.
Her phone buzzes. The smile drops off her face for one beat.

Rules of thumb

One page ≈ one minuteA feature runs 90 to 120 pages; a half-hour pilot about 30.
Present tense, active voice"She opens the door", never "She is opening".
Only what we can see or hearNo thoughts, no backstory in action lines. Show it, or let someone say it.
White space is paceShort lines read fast. Dense blocks slow the read and the film.
Courier 12, standard marginsOScreenplay does the layout; export to Fountain or print to PDF.
Don't direct the cameraWrite the moment; a shot only when the story needs that exact image.
Special formatsMONTAGE, SERIES OF SHOTS, INTERCUT, FLASHBACK, SUPER: (text on screen), PRE-LAP (sound before the cut).
Name your scenes for the readerKeep location names consistent, so the reader always knows where they are.

In the Pages tab, Enter and Tab move between elements the way professional software does: after a character name comes dialogue, Tab on an empty action line makes a character, and typing INT. turns a line into a scene heading.

The Story Circle

Dan Harmon, co-creator of Rick and Morty and Community, boiled Joseph Campbell's hero's journey down to eight steps on a circle. His writers' rooms famously break episodes on it: a full circle can fit in eleven minutes or two hours, and the A story and B story can each run their own.

Why it works

  • Order above, chaos below. Steps 1 and 2 (and 8) are the familiar world; steps 3 to 7 descend into the unfamiliar and climb back out. Every story is a trip down and back up.
  • Opposites face each other. You (1) and Find (5) mirror each other, as do Need (2) and Take (6), Go (3) and Return (7), Search (4) and Change (8). If step 5 doesn't echo step 1, the circle wobbles.
  • Get, then pay. The hero gets what they wanted at the halfway point, and it costs them. That cost is what changes them.
  • It's fractal. A whole film, an act, a scene, even a single joke can run the circle.

Try it: the There Is No Bull sample is an eight-scene circle that grew from a single Spark. Every project shows its scene cards on the wheel, so an empty step is impossible to miss.

What The Godfather teaches

Francis Ford Coppola and Mario Puzo's 1972 film is studied in film schools for its structure, its scenes and its arc. Here are eleven lessons you can use tomorrow, scene by scene. (Watch the film; the lessons describe it rather than quote it.)

1

Open with a ritual that introduces everyone

The film begins at a wedding: a bright public party outside, private business in a dark office inside. Every guest who visits the head of the family wants something, so each character is introduced through a request.

Use it: Introduce characters through what they want, and set two worlds against each other in one location.

2

Make the hero's starting position unmistakable

Michael arrives in uniform, with an outsider, and explains his family to her from a distance. His stance is clear: that's them, not me. The whole film is the measured destruction of that sentence.

Use it: State where the protagonist starts as clearly as you can. An arc is only visible against a clear starting point.

3

The threshold can be a quiet act of care

At the hospital, Michael finds his father unprotected and decides to stand guard. No gun is fired. He simply chooses the family for one night, and the audience goes with him because the choice is loving.

Use it: Let the point of no return be an action the audience agrees with. They'll follow your hero into the dark.

4

Suspense is information plus waiting

Before the restaurant scene, we learn exactly where a hidden gun will be. So every second of the dinner is unbearable: will it be there, will he do it? The conversation runs in a language without subtitles, and a passing train swells on the soundtrack as he decides.

Use it: Tell the audience what's at stake before the characters act, then make them wait. Use sound to make an inner state audible.

5

One image beats a threat speech

A powerful man refuses a favour and wakes up to an image so shocking it needs no dialogue at all. The family's reach is established for the rest of the film in a single shot.

Use it: Escalate with an image, not an explanation. The audience will never forget it, and nobody has to say the threat out loud.

6

Supporting characters as roads not taken

Each brother is a version of what Michael could become: one ruled by temper, one by weakness, and the adopted son who has the logic but not the blood. Each fails exactly the way his nature predicts.

Use it: Build the supporting cast as mirrors of your hero's possible paths. The Web tab is built for this.

7

A mentor's last scene is a setup

In a quiet garden, the retired father tells Michael how the betrayal will come: whoever brings him a peace meeting is the traitor. Then he dies playing with his grandson. Later the prediction comes true, word for word.

Use it: Give the mentor's final scene a plant, so their death leaves the hero alone with knowledge they must use.

8

Cross-cut so each action comments on the other

The baptism: Michael renounces evil at the altar while, across the city, his enemies are killed on his orders. Sacrament and murder intercut. He becomes a godfather in both senses at once.

Use it: Intercut two actions to make the theme visible without anyone stating it.

9

End on a point of view

The last scene answers the first. Kay asks the question; Michael lies; a door closes between her and the dark office, seen from her side. The hero has fully entered the room the film opened in.

Use it: Let the final image mirror the opening image, changed. Choose whose eyes we end in.

10

A negative arc works when every step is reasonable

From war hero who wants no part to head of the family, each of Michael's choices makes sense from inside. We half agree with every step, which is why the destination horrifies us.

Use it: For a corruption arc, motivate every step so well that the audience is complicit.

11

Think in movements

The film moves in self-contained sequences: the wedding, the shooting, the hospital, the restaurant, the exile, the return, the baptism. Each has its own question and its own climax.

Use it: The Eight Sequences structure in OScreenplay builds exactly this. The Salt Debt sample uses it.

Character craft

A character is a set of pressures. OScreenplay asks for the ones that produce behaviour:

Woundwhat happened→Liewhat they believe→Flawhow it shows→Wantwhat they chase↔Needwhat would heal them
  • Want and need pull apart. The want drives the plot (get the money, win the duel); the need drives the arc (accept help, stop running). The climax forces a choice between them.
  • Arcs come in three shapes. Positive: the hero unlearns the lie. Negative: the hero doubles down and is corrupted (Michael Corleone). Flat: the hero already knows the truth and changes the world around them.
  • Personality is a tool, not a horoscope. The Big Five (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism) predict how people react under stress. The reaction engine in every character uses them, with the flaw, fear and values, to suggest what that person would logically do.
  • Every villain is the hero of their own story. Give the antagonist a want, a wound and a reason they think they're right.
  • Voice is character. Cover the names on a page of dialogue: can you still tell who's talking?
  • Conditions and illnesses belong to people, not plots. Research them, talk to people who live with them, and never use them as a punchline or a twist.

Scene craft

  • Goal, conflict, outcome. Someone wants something in this scene; someone or something is in the way; it ends with "yes, but", "no, and", or a clean win or loss.
  • Every scene turns. A value (safety, love, hope) starts positive or negative and ends differently. If nothing changed, the scene can go. Each scene card has a charge in and a charge out; the Storyline draws them as your story's shape.
  • Arrive late, leave early. Skip the hellos and the goodbyes; cut in when the conflict is already running.
  • Two jobs per scene. Move the plot and reveal character, or plant a setup and pay off another.
  • Alternate. Up, down, up. Four bleak scenes in a row numb an audience; the doctor flags it.
  • Tell it out of order on purpose. The Chronology holds what happened; the Storyline holds what we see. A flash-forward opening, a flashback at the midpoint: the braid shows each one.

Stuck? The scene recipes and the complications deck are made for this.

Dialogue

  • People rarely say what they mean. Write the surface, and let the subtext do the work. "Sit." can mean "I love you", "obey me" and "I'm dying".
  • Never let characters tell each other what they both know. If the audience needs it, create a reason for someone not to know it.
  • Conflict in every exchange. Even friends disagree about something small.
  • Short beats long. Interruptions, unfinished sentences and silence are dialogue too.
  • Read it out loud. Your ear catches what your eye forgives.

Glossary

LoglineThe story in one sentence: hero, goal, obstacle, stakes.
PremiseThe "what if" behind the story.
Inciting incidentThe event that starts the story; it happens TO the hero.
Plot pointA turn that sends the story in a new direction, usually at the act breaks.
MidpointThe middle turn: the hero goes from reacting to acting, or a false victory becomes a defeat.
WantThe outer goal: what the character is trying to get.
NeedThe inner truth they must learn, often the opposite of what they want.
Wound (ghost)The past hurt that shaped them.
LieThe false belief the wound planted. A positive arc unlearns it.
ArcHow a character changes (positive), is corrupted (negative) or changes others while staying the same (flat).
BeatA unit of story. In a beat sheet, a structural moment; inside a scene, a single exchange of action and reaction.
SequenceA run of scenes with its own mini-story, about 10 to 15 pages.
Setup and payoffPlant something early, use it later. Also "Chekhov's gun".
SubtextWhat a line really means under what it says.
On the noseDialogue that says exactly what the character feels. Usually a problem.
Value chargeThe emotional value at stake in a scene (love, safety, freedom) and whether it ends positive or negative. Every scene should turn it.
SluglineA scene heading: INT./EXT. LOCATION - TIME.
V.O. / O.S.Voice-over (narration, not in the scene) and off-screen (in the scene, out of sight).
Cold openA TV teaser before the titles.
ButtonA last line or image that closes a scene with a snap.
FountainA plain-text screenplay format that most screenwriting apps can open.
Spec scriptA script written on speculation, not commissioned.
CoverageA reader's report on a script for a producer.
MacGuffinThe object everyone wants; it matters because they want it.
Fabula and syuzhetThe events in the order they happened, and the order the story tells them. OScreenplay's Chronology and Storyline.
